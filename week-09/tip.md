# Tips for this week

This week I focused on the user interface, user experience, and accessibility of the application, with particular attention to how suitable it would be for the types of users expected in an NHS environment. At this stage, I was thinking less about simply adding more features and more about whether the system would actually be easy to understand and use for patients and visitors who may be elderly, stressed, unfamiliar with technology, or have additional accessibility needs. This was important because a navigation system in a hospital has to be clear and supportive under pressure, not just technically functional.

A major part of my thinking this week was around making the application simpler and more intuitive. I considered how the layout, button placement, text, and general flow of the pages could affect how easily a user could complete tasks such as selecting their location, choosing a destination, and following directions. For this project, good UI/UX meant reducing confusion and keeping the journey through the application as straightforward as possible. I wanted the design to feel predictable and easy to follow, especially for users who might already be anxious or unfamiliar with hospital layouts.

Accessibility was also a key part of my contribution this week. I had already worked on features such as text resizing, screen reading support, and language options, but this week helped me reflect more critically on how those choices affected the overall usability of the application. I considered how features such as high contrast, larger text, reduced motion, read-aloud support, and accessible route options could make the system more inclusive for a wider range of users. I also thought about the importance of not treating accessibility as a separate extra, but as something that should be built into the experience from the beginning.
Another important area was how translated content and interface design worked together. Because the project involved multilingual support, I had to think about whether translated text would still remain readable, clearly placed, and understandable within the interface. This made me reflect on how accessibility is not only about visual or physical needs, but also about helping users overcome language barriers in a practical and reliable way.

Overall, this week helped me think more critically about software quality from the user’s point of view. Rather than just asking whether the application worked, I was asking whether it worked well for the people it was designed for. Looking back, this week was important because it reinforced that strong software design in a healthcare setting depends on clear UI/UX decisions, accessible features, and a strong awareness of the real context in which the system will be used.
